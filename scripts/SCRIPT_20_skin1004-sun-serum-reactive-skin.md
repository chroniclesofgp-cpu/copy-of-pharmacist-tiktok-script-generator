## Full Script

**[HOOK]**
If your skin gets red after being in the sun — you are using the wrong sunscreen.

**[AUTHORITY]**
I'm a pharmacist. I counsel patients on skincare every day, and this is one of the most common things I see people get wrong.

**[PROBLEM — Symptom List]**
Most sunscreens do one thing — they block UV radiation. That is it. They do nothing for what happens inside your skin after UV hits it.

When UV radiation reaches your skin, it triggers an inflammatory response. Your skin releases prostaglandins — the same chemical messengers that cause swelling and redness when you get a cut. If you have reactive skin, rosacea, or post-procedure skin, that response is amplified. The result: redness, flushing, sensitivity, and that tight, irritated feeling after a day outside.

*(On-screen overlay: symptom list — "redness after sun / flushing / tight irritated feeling / sensitive skin")*

**[GAP]**
The gap is that most people think SPF is the whole story. It is not. Blocking UV is step one. Calming the inflammatory response that UV triggers is step two — and almost no sunscreen addresses both.

**[EDUCATION — Mechanism]**
This is where Centella Asiatica comes in. Centella contains two compounds — madecassoside and asiaticoside. These are triterpenoid saponins, and they work as natural COX inhibitors.

*(On-screen overlay: "Centella Asiatica → madecassoside + asiaticoside → COX inhibitor")*

COX — cyclooxygenase — is the same enzyme that ibuprofen blocks. When you inhibit COX, you interrupt the prostaglandin cascade. Less prostaglandins means less redness, less swelling, less of that post-sun inflammatory response.

*(On-screen overlay: study popup — Ratz-Łyko A et al. 2016, Indian J Pharm Sci, PMC4852572 — "Centella asiatica inhibits cyclooxygenase — the same anti-inflammatory pathway as NSAIDs")*

This is not a marketing claim. This is documented biochemistry.
