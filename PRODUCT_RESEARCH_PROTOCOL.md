# Product Research Protocol
## RxContent — Pharmacist TikTok Script Generator

**Purpose:** This document defines the two-tier research protocol used every time a new product is introduced. It ensures no valuable ingredient, clinical study, or customer insight is missed before scripts are written. The Oligonol discovery in the Bloom vs. Loaded Tea project is the reference example for why this protocol exists — a patented ingredient with 30 clinical trials was sitting on the label and would have been described as generic marketing language without the deep dive.

**Protocol Files:** Every product that completes a Full Protocol gets its own saved intelligence doc at `/home/ubuntu/pharma-script-gen/product-intel/[product-name]-intel.md`. This doc is referenced every time a new script is written for that product — no re-research needed.

---

## When to Use Each Protocol

| Situation | Protocol |
|---|---|
| Evaluating a product before deciding to promote it | **Light** |
| Product is a secondary mention in a comparison (not the hero) | **Light** |
| Product you have committed to promoting — affiliate link secured or product in hand | **Full** |
| Second or third script for a product already researched | Reference existing intel doc — no new research |
| Product sent as a TikTok Shop link with no context | **Light** first — upgrade to Full if you decide to move forward |

# LIGHT PROTOCOL

**Time required:** 10–15 minutes
**Output:** Short notes block saved at the top of the script file (not a standalone doc)
**Purpose:** Quickly assess whether a product is worth promoting and flag anything obviously useful for content

---

## Step 1 — Verify the Label

Go to the TikTok Shop listing or brand website. Pull the full ingredient list (INCI). Record:
- All active ingredients
- Any patented or branded ingredients (look for ™ or ® symbols next to ingredient names — these are always worth flagging)
- Free-from claims (aluminum-free, paraben-free, etc.)
- Certifications if listed

**Flag immediately:** Any ingredient with a ™ or ® symbol. Patented ingredients almost always have clinical data behind them. This is the single most important thing to look for in the Light Protocol.

---

## Step 2 — Check the Brand's Own Claims

Go to the brand's product page. Read:
- The headline claim (what they say the product does in one line)
- The key benefits section
- The "how it works" or ingredient callout section if present

**Why this matters:** The brand spent money on marketing research. Their claims tell you what their target customer cares about most. If they lead with "brightens dark underarms in 4 weeks" — that is the pain point. If they lead with "no irritation" — that is the objection they are countering. Use their own framing as a starting point.

---

## Step 3 — Quick Review Scan

Check TikTok Shop reviews (or Amazon if TikTok Shop reviews are thin). Look at:
- The most common reason people say they bought it (the pain point)
- The most common complaint or hesitation in negative reviews
- Star rating and total number of reviews/sales

**Note any recurring objection** — this is what your viewer is thinking while watching your video. Addressing it directly is one of the most effective conversion techniques.

---

## Step 4 — Light Assessment

Based on Steps 1–3, answer:
- Is there at least one ingredient worth explaining on camera?
- Is there a patented ingredient that needs a Full Protocol deep dive?
- Is the product credible enough to promote?
- What is the primary pain point this product addresses?

**Output:** Write a short notes block (5–10 lines) at the top of the script file. If a patented ingredient was found, flag it for Full Protocol research before writing the script.

# FULL PROTOCOL

**Time required:** 30–60 minutes
**Output:** Standalone product intelligence doc saved at `/home/ubuntu/pharma-script-gen/product-intel/[product-name]-intel.md`
**Purpose:** Build a permanent, comprehensive intelligence file that can be referenced for every script written about this product — forever

---

## Step 1 — Full Ingredient Verification

Go to the TikTok Shop listing AND the brand's official website. Pull the complete INCI ingredient list from both sources and cross-reference for consistency. Record:
- Full INCI list in order (order matters — ingredients are listed highest to lowest concentration)
- All active ingredients with their known mechanisms
- Every patented or branded ingredient (™ or ® symbols)
- Free-from claims
- Certifications (dermatologist tested, cruelty-free, etc.)
- Concentration if disclosed (e.g., "5% AHA," "2% kojic acid")

---

## Step 2 — Patented Ingredient Deep Dive

For every patented or branded ingredient found in Step 1:

**2a. Identify the ingredient.** Search the ingredient name + "patented ingredient" or the brand name behind the patent. Example: "Oligonol lychee extract" or "Sunfiber guar bean fiber." The brand name is almost always findable via the ingredient supplier's website or a supplement industry database.

**2b. Find the clinical evidence.** Search PubMed (pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) for the ingredient name. Look for:
- Human clinical trials (randomized controlled trials preferred)
- The specific mechanism studied
- The dose used in the study
- The outcome measured

**2c. Record the best study for on-screen use.** The ideal study for a popup overlay is: human RCT, specific outcome with a number (e.g., "7.4% increase in anaerobic threshold"), published in a named journal. Record: Author, year, journal, DOI, key finding in one sentence.

**2d. Note the dose caveat.** If the product does not disclose how much of the patented ingredient is in the formula, note this. You can cite the mechanism and the research — you cannot claim the product delivers the same outcome as the study dose.

---

## Step 3 — Active Ingredient Mechanism Research

For each non-patented active ingredient (kojic acid, AHA, niacinamide, ceramide, etc.):
- Confirm the mechanism (what it does at a cellular or biochemical level)
- Find at least one supporting study on PubMed for on-screen use
- Note whether the mechanism is relevant to the primary pain point this product addresses

**Priority order for research depth:** Ingredients listed highest in the INCI (higher concentration) and ingredients the brand calls out in their marketing get the deepest research. Filler or carrier ingredients (water, glycerin, etc.) do not need research.

---

## Step 4 — Brand Marketing Analysis

Go to the brand's product page, their TikTok account, and any paid ad content you can find. Record:
- The headline claim and primary benefit they lead with
- Which ingredients they call out by name in marketing
- What customer problem they frame the product as solving
- Any clinical claims they make (e.g., "clinically tested," "dermatologist approved")
- The price point and how they position it (premium, value, clinical, etc.)

**Why this matters:** The brand's marketing tells you what their research shows converts. Their ingredient callouts tell you what their customers respond to. You are not copying their marketing — you are understanding the customer psychology behind it so you can address the same pain points with pharmacist-level credibility.

---

## Step 5 — Review Mining

Check all three sources if available: TikTok Shop, Amazon, and the brand's own website.

For each source, record:
- **Top reason people buy** (the primary pain point — usually in 5-star reviews)
- **Most common complaint** (product limitation, side effect, or unmet expectation)
- **Most common pre-purchase hesitation** (questions in Q&A sections, skeptical reviews)
- **Who is buying it** (skin type, age group, specific condition mentioned repeatedly)
- **Any unexpected use case** (people using it for something the brand did not advertise)

**The objection list is the most valuable output of this step.** Every objection that appears repeatedly in reviews is sitting in your viewer's head while they watch your video. Addressing it directly — before they think to ask — is one of the highest-leverage conversion techniques available.

---

## Step 6 — Competitive Decision Map

Build the real buyer decision set before a product is placed in a comparison, Tier List, scorecard, numeric rating, or best-last list. Research the 2–5 **recognizable alternatives** a viewer actually uses, considers, or is frustrated by. They may be competitive products, ingredients/forms, procedures, professional treatments, alternative therapies, routinely recommended solutions, or relevant routines—not only products that can be linked.

For every selected alternative, record:
- **Why viewers know or use it** — market/category presence, review language, creator-content frequency, patient/question frequency, or another current source.
- **Its narrow useful role** — who it may fit or what part of the problem it can address.
- **The real recurring limitation or friction** — source-supported cost, inconvenience, narrow use case, side-effect concern, slow timeline, maintenance burden, coverage gap, or common complaint. Do not invent a flaw.
- **Where the researched product fits** — the specific viewer goal or routine gap it addresses, without saying it replaces every other option.
- **Link eligibility** — whether the item is current and available in TikTok Shop. More than one 8/10+ product may be linked when the products have distinct, clearly explained use cases.

**Required output — Competitive Decision Map:**

| Alternative | Type | Why viewers know/use it | Narrow role | Source-supported limitation/frustration | Where researched product fits | Link status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Option] | Product / procedure / therapy / routine | [Evidence] | [Role] | [Evidence] | [Gap] | Linked / unlinked / not applicable |

This is more than a short comparison note. It prevents forced rankings, generic unnamed competitors, and unfair attacks while giving decision formats the exact familiar tension that makes them watchable.

---

## Step 7 — Competitor Content Audit (TikTok)

Search TikTok for the product name or primary ingredient + "pharmacist" or "doctor." Look at the top 5–10 videos from credentialed healthcare creators. For each video found, open the video and record the direct URL.

For each video found, record:
- **Creator handle**
- **Video URL** (direct TikTok link — required for reference during filming)
- **Hook type used** (comparison, instruction/correction, trend or trash, symptom checklist, etc.)
- **View count and estimated GMV** (if visible via TikTok Shop affiliate dashboard or Kalodata — high GMV = proven angle)
- **What they said about the product** (key claims, angles taken)
- **What they did NOT say** (gaps = potential pharmacist-only angles)
- **What to replicate** (specific hook phrasing, structural element, or angle that worked)
- **What to differentiate** (where the pharmacist version adds exclusive value they could not provide)

**The goal of this step is two things:**
1. **Find what is proven.** High-view videos on this product are the first scripts to replicate and iterate on. A proven hook on a proven product is always the starting point — not a reason to avoid it.
2. **Find what is open.** Angles that no credentialed creator has taken yet are opportunities to own a lane. These are the test videos after the proven ones.

**Output format — required table:**

| Rank | Creator | Video URL | Hook Type | Views/GMV | Replicate | Differentiate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | @handle | [link](url) | hook-type | views / $GMV | what worked | pharmacist angle |

Ranked by GMV first, then view count. Minimum 3 videos documented. If fewer than 3 healthcare creator videos exist for this product, note that and document the top general creator videos instead.

**These video URLs carry forward into the campaign doc.** For each campaign video planned in Step 12, the closest reference video from this table must be linked in the campaign table as "Reference Video" so the creator can watch it immediately before filming.

---

## Step 8 — Comment Intelligence

Go to the top 2–3 TikTok videos about this product (from Step 7 or from general search). Read the comments.

Record:
- **Most common questions asked** (these are hooks — people asking a question in comments are the same people who would stop scrolling to hear the answer)
- **Most common objections raised in comments** (price, skepticism, side effects, "does this actually work")
- **Any specific use case or population mentioned repeatedly** ("does this work for dark spots from shaving?" — that is a script angle)
- **Any misconception being repeated** (correction hooks come from misconceptions)

**Why comments beat reviews:** Reviews capture post-purchase sentiment. Comments capture mid-consideration thinking — the exact moment when someone is deciding whether to buy. That is the viewer you are talking to.

---

## Step 9 — Pharmacist-Only Angle

This is the most important output of the Full Protocol. Ask explicitly:

**What is the one thing a pharmacist can say about this product that no regular influencer can?**

This could be:
- A drug interaction or contraindication ("if you take [medication], talk to your pharmacist before using this")
- A mechanism explanation that requires clinical training to understand and explain credibly
- A dosing insight (the study used X dose — the product has Y — here is what that means)
- A population-specific warning (not for pregnant women, not for people with [condition])
- A comparison to a pharmaceutical equivalent ("this ingredient works the same way as [drug class] but without the side effects")
- An ingredient interaction within the formula itself (like the Bloom sucralose/prebiotic contradiction)

**This field must be filled in.** If nothing is found, write "none identified" — but always look. The Oligonol discovery, the sucralose/prebiotic contradiction, and the MK-7 bioavailability point are all examples of pharmacist-only angles that were found because someone looked.

---

## Step 10 — Contraindications and Interaction Flags

For any product with active ingredients (supplements, skincare actives, caffeine-containing products):

- Are there populations who should NOT use this product? (pregnant, nursing, children, specific conditions)
- Are there known drug interactions? (e.g., vitamin K2 and blood thinners, caffeine and certain medications)
- Are there ingredient interactions within the formula that a pharmacist would flag?
- Is there a maximum safe dose or frequency of use?

**These are not reasons to avoid promoting the product.** They are pharmacist-level content angles. A line like *"if you take blood thinners, talk to your pharmacist before adding K2"* builds more trust than any ingredient claim. It signals that you actually know what you are talking about — and it protects your credibility.

Record any flags found. If none exist, write "none identified."

---

## Step 11 — Content Angle Inventory

Based on everything gathered in Steps 1–10, list every content angle this product supports:

- **Proven angles** (from Step 7 — what has already worked for other credentialed creators — replicate these first)
- **Open angles** (from Step 7 — what no credentialed creator has done yet — test these after proven ones)
- **Ingredient education angles** (explain what an ingredient does and why it matters)
- **Mechanism angles** (explain how the product works at a scientific level)
- **Comparison angles** (this vs. a competing product)
- **Protocol angles** (how to use this product correctly or in combination with another)
- **Objection-busting angles** (address the most common hesitation directly — from Step 5 and Step 8)
- **Pharmacist authority angles** (from Step 9 — the angles only a pharmacist can take)
- **Contraindication/interaction angles** (from Step 10 — trust-building pharmacist warnings)
- **Study popup angles** (any claim that can be backed with an on-screen citation)

This list becomes the source for future script ideas. Every angle listed here is a potential video. **Always start with proven angles. Test open angles after.**

---

## Step 12 — Content Campaign Plan

Based on everything gathered in Steps 1–11, design a phased content launch sequence for this product. The goal is to move a viewer from cold awareness (TOF) through consideration (MOF) to purchase (BOF) across multiple videos — not to close every viewer in a single video.

**Funnel stage definitions:**
- **TOF (Top of Funnel):** Viewer has never heard of this ingredient or product category. Hook must be broad, relatable, or curiosity-driven. No assumed knowledge.
- **MOF (Middle of Funnel):** Viewer knows the category exists and is considering options. Hook can reference the ingredient by name. Education and comparison content lives here.
- **BOF (Bottom of Funnel):** Viewer is ready to buy but needs a final push. Hook can reference the specific product. Objection-busting, proof stacks, and scarcity CTAs live here.

**How to complete this step:**

For each video, identify:
1. **The hook type** — which hook framework from `HOOK_FRAMEWORKS.md` fits best for this stage and product
2. **The content angle** — one specific angle from the Content Angle Inventory (Step 11) that matches this stage
3. **The primary psychological lever** — from `BUYER_PSYCHOLOGY_LEVERS.md`, which lever is most appropriate for this stage
4. **The CTA style** — which of the four proven CTA styles to use (see below)

**CTA rule — non-negotiable:** Every video at every funnel stage ends with a sell CTA pointing to the orange cart or product link. This is confirmed 5/5 creators across 84 videos. There is no "awareness only" or "engagement only" video in the dataset — every video is designed to close. The difference between TOF and BOF is the hook and education depth, not whether a sell CTA is included.

**The four proven CTA styles (from 84-video analysis):**
- **Stock scarcity close:** *"If you see that orange cart, that means they still have it in stock."* (naturo, 6/9 videos nearly verbatim; rphreviews variant: *"click the orange cart below before it runs out"*)
- **Flash sale close:** *"They're running a flash sale right now — I'll leave the link on the bottom of the screen."* (Drew, 20/20 videos; all 5 creators use this)
- **Soft recommendation close:** *"I would grab it if you can — I wouldn't wait on this one."* (Riva, Dr. Faith — warm persona variant)
- **Comment invite + sell:** *"If you have any questions, let me know in the comments — and I'll leave the link below."* (Drew, 8/20 videos as secondary CTA after the sell CTA)

**Physical gesture — non-negotiable:** Downward-pointing finger gesture synchronized with verbal CTA. Confirmed 5/5 creators. Always pair with a red or orange arrow graphic overlay pointing to the cart icon.

**Sequencing rule:** Always produce at least one TOF video before running MOF or BOF content. TOF builds the audience that MOF and BOF convert. A product with only BOF content will plateau quickly.

**Minimum campaign plan:** 1 TOF video + 2 MOF videos + 1 BOF video (4 videos minimum). This is the minimum viable campaign for any new product.

**BOF suite — required for every campaign:** In addition to the minimum 4-video sequence, every campaign must include all 4 BOF suite scripts from `BOF_INSTRUCTION_CORRECTION_TEMPLATES.md`:
- **Type 1** — Short-form negative apology ("my patients are returning this" — pain point benefit stack, no science)
- **Type 2** — Longer-form negative apology (same hook, each benefit line gets one education sentence — pharmacist mechanism explanation)
- **Type 3** — Instruction-correction ("they shoved this down your throat" — how-to-use gap filler)
- **Type 4** — Scam-warning + label-reading ("this is fake, this is a scam, and this is fake" — only use when 3 verifiable label differentiators exist and/or knockoff listings are present)

The BOF suite runs after the initial TOF/MOF sequence is live. All 4 types are required in the campaign plan. Type 4 should be noted as conditional — include it if the product has 3+ label-readable differentiators that most competitors fail; flag it as "pending viability check" if not yet confirmed.

**Proven campaign sequences from the 5-creator dataset:**
- **rphreviews pattern:** TOF (broad symptom hook) → MOF (mechanism explainer) → MOF (comparison) → BOF (right-way how-to with CTA)
- **Dr. Faith pattern:** MOF (specific audience situation hook) → BOF (bait-and-switch trust architecture with CTA)
- **naturo pattern:** MOF (A vs. B comparison) → BOF (bundle recommendation with orange cart CTA)
- **Drew pattern:** TOF (story/news stitch) → MOF (PubMed wall mechanism) → BOF (expert verdict with CTA)

Record the campaign plan in the intel doc. Update it as videos are produced and performance data comes in.

## Protocol Trigger Rules (Summary)

These rules apply every time a product is introduced — whether via a TikTok Shop link, a brand website, or a product mentioned in conversation:

1. **Any new product link received → run Light Protocol immediately.** Do not write a script without at least the Light Protocol completed.
2. **Any patented ingredient found in Light Protocol → flag for Full Protocol before writing the script.** Patented ingredients are the highest-value research targets.
3. **Any product committed to for promotion → run Full Protocol before the first script is written.** The intel doc is built once and referenced forever.
4. **Any script for a product with an existing intel doc → reference the intel doc.** Do not re-research what is already documented.
5. **Any factual claim in a script → must be traceable to a verified source in the intel doc or the Light Protocol notes.** No ingredient claims from memory or assumption.
6. **Any set or bundle product → verify the full product contents manually before writing the intel doc.** Parallel research subtasks cannot reliably confirm complete set contents from TikTok Shop listing pages alone. For any set or bundle, navigate directly to the brand website or a third-party retailer (Ulta, Amazon, Sephora) to confirm every product in the set before writing. Do not write the intel doc until the full contents are confirmed.
7. **Intel doc completeness check before delivery.** Before writing any intel doc, confirm all three of the following: (a) Full INCI ingredient list sourced from INCIDecoder, DailyMed, or the brand website — not just the TikTok Shop listing. (b) For sets and bundles — every product in the set is named and has its own ingredient breakdown. No placeholder language like "additional products" or "other ingredients" is acceptable. (c) All PubMed citations are verified via NCBI E-utilities before being written into the doc. Any PMID that cannot be confirmed is excluded.

8. **Non-skincare products require category-specific verification — INCIDecoder/CosDNA do not apply.** Every product type has a primary source of truth for its formula. Use the appropriate source for the product category before writing any intel doc or script. The verification source must be cited in the intel doc. Acceptable sources by category:

   | Product Category | Primary Verification Source | Secondary Source |
   |---|---|---|
   | Skincare / cosmetics | INCIDecoder.com, CosDNA.com, SkinSort.com | Brand website INCI panel |
   | Supplements / vitamins / gummies | FDA Supplement Facts panel (from brand website or Amazon listing) — full ingredient list including inactive/other ingredients | Labdoor.com, Examine.com for mechanism verification |
   | Energy drinks / beverages | Nutrition Facts + Supplement Facts panel from brand website or Amazon | FDA GRAS database for novel ingredients |
   | Oral care (mouthwash, toothpaste, whitening) | Drug Facts panel (OTC) or Inactive Ingredients panel from brand website | FDA OTC monograph for active ingredient claims |
   | Chewing gum with actives (e.g., Neuro Gum) | Supplement Facts panel from brand website — verify each active ingredient dose | Examine.com or PubMed for dose-efficacy verification |
   | Deodorant / antiperspirant | Drug Facts panel (OTC) or full ingredient list from brand website | INCIDecoder if listed |

   **The rule is the same regardless of category:** every ingredient named in a script must be traceable to a verified label or panel from a primary source. No ingredient from memory, assumption, or "typical formula" reasoning. If the primary source cannot be found, state that in the intel doc and do not write ingredient-specific claims into the script until it is located.

   **Added June 22, 2026** — triggered by retroactive audit finding that Bloom Sparkling Energy, Celsius, Hismile, Neuro Gum, and Toplux Magnesium intel docs could not be verified against skincare databases. Non-skincare products are equally at risk for ingredient fabrication errors and require the same verification standard applied through their category-appropriate source.

## Script-Writing Guidance: Tiered Ingredient Naming

When writing a script, ingredients should be named in this priority order. This rule applies to both spoken delivery and on-screen text overlays.

**Tier 1 — Brand-promoted ingredients (say verbally on camera)**
Whatever the brand features on their TikTok Shop listing, Amazon listing, or product page hero section. These are the ingredients the viewer has most likely already seen in ads. Name them verbally — they carry pre-built trust.

**Tier 2 — Patented or clinically studied ingredients (say verbally on camera)**
Even if the brand undermarkets them. These are the Oligonol-type finds. Name them verbally because they are the pharmacist-only insight — the thing no regular creator can say with credibility. This is the highest-value spoken content.

**Tier 3 — Supporting or barrier-repair ingredients (text overlay only)**
Real ingredients that add to the value stack but are not the primary story. Ceramides, emollients, anti-inflammatories, botanical extracts. These belong in the text overlays — they show depth without consuming spoken time on names the viewer does not recognize.

**The rule in practice:**
- Do not skip Tier 1 ingredients to name Tier 3 ones — the brand promoted Tier 1 because their research shows those convert.
- Do not eliminate Tier 3 ingredients — they are how you value-stack and show the product does more than the brand says. They just live in overlays, not in the spoken script.
- If a Tier 3 ingredient has a patented or clinical angle (like Oligonol), it becomes Tier 2 and moves to spoken delivery.

**Why this matters:** The ceramide NP / shea butter / Centella Asiatica error in the Medicube deodorant scripts is the reference example. Those are real ingredients in the formula — but the brand promotes niacinamide and vitamin C. The scripts named the wrong tier verbally. The fix: niacinamide and vitamin C spoken on camera, ceramide/Centella in the overlay.

## Reference Example

**The Oligonol Discovery (Bloom Sparkling Energy — May 2026)**

The original Bloom vs. Loaded Tea script described the lychee extract as "patented lychee extract" — accurate but generic. The Light Protocol flagged the ™ symbol on the ingredient. The Full Protocol identified the ingredient as Oligonol (MayPro/Amino Up), found 30 human clinical trials, confirmed the mechanism (nitric oxide → improved circulation → reduced fatigue), and identified the key study for on-screen use (Kang SW et al., 2012, *J Clin Biochem Nutr*, 7.4% increase in anaerobic threshold, double-blind RCT, 70 participants). The on-camera line became: *"patented lychee extract backed by 30 clinical trials — it increases nitric oxide, which improves circulation and reduces fatigue."* This is the difference the Full Protocol makes.
