# DryWater Raspberry Lemon — Purchase-Reason Reassessment

**Purpose:** Re-rank the strongest **source-supported reasons to choose the exact Raspberry Lemon 12-stick product** after the first campaign overused generic hydration context. This is a planning-analysis document, not a script or a promise of product efficacy.

> **Key conclusion:** The strongest DryWater sale is not “water is bad” or “1,000 mg potassium fixes hydration.” It is a **clean, fully disclosed, independently certified, fruit-forward stick formula** for a buyer who is tired of sugary, artificial-tasting, or opaque hydration powders. Electrolyte amounts and the whole-formula count then validate the purchase; they should not crowd out the product’s actual consumer appeal.

## What the research changes

The user-observed **clean ingredients, no artificial flavors, real fruit, and no-added-sugar** claims are not merely reasonable selling points; the exact Raspberry Lemon evidence supports them. The research also identifies one stronger, more differentiated proof asset that the first package missed: **the exact Raspberry Lemon product is independently listed by NSF Certified for Sport.** That certification has a clear consumer translation—“the label is checked against the product, and the program screens for banned substances and unsafe contaminants”—without making a medical claim. [1] [2]

The campaign should make the following sequence central: **the buyer’s current packet is too sugary, artificial-tasting, or opaque → DryWater’s exact label gives them a cleaner fruit-forward alternative → the panel proves what is in it → NSF certification adds an independent trust signal → the stick format makes it easy to actually keep using.** The potassium amount should act as one distinct panel fact, not as the entire product story.

## Objections and honest trade-offs

| Objection or limitation | Evidence | How it should improve the selling—not dilute it |
|---|---|---|
| “Will I like the taste?” | Reviews praise less-sweet/fresh taste, but taste is subjective and one shopper disliked Lemon Lime. [4] | Show the Raspberry Lemon mix and describe the **formula preference**, not a universal taste result. Use “not too sweet” only as a review quote or buyer preference. |
| “Is this for every workout or all-day every day?” | Category evidence makes hydration and electrolyte needs contextual; the product’s 1,000-mg potassium panel is material for people with certain kidney conditions or medication regimens. [7] | Let the target use case do the narrowing. One instruction asset can include the brief relevant check; repeating it elsewhere is not helpful product education. |
| “Why not use a high-sodium packet?” | LMNT legitimately serves a high-sodium/low-carb use case and discloses 1,000 mg sodium, 200 mg potassium, and 60 mg magnesium, with stevia leaf extract in flavored drink mixes. [8] | This creates a distinct comparison use case: DryWater for the fruit-forward, no-artificial, zero-added-sugar, higher-potassium formula shopper; LMNT for the person intentionally looking for a very high-sodium/low-carb formula. |
| “Is it a performance fuel?” | Independent review notes that endurance activity may require carbohydrate fuel beyond a low-calorie hydration mix; that source is sponsored and therefore only a context lead, not proof of product benefit. [9] | Do not sell DryWater as a replacement for food, gels, or endurance fuel. This protects the product-positioning lane from becoming a claim the formula cannot support. |

## Locked product-message hierarchy for the rewrite

Every core asset must sell **one distinct priority** from this list. Supporting facts may reinforce it, but cannot duplicate the message from another script.

1. **Independent quality/label trust:** exact Raspberry Lemon NSF Certified for Sport listing and what the certification actually checks.
2. **Clean formula, without the candy-packet tradeoff:** 0 g added sugar; no artificial flavors, colors, or sweeteners per the exact NSF listing.
3. **Fruit-forward, transparent composition:** four named fruit sources; disclosed electrolytes/minerals, vitamins, and glycine—shown on the exact panel.
4. **Clear buyer-specific comparison win:** DryWater over Original Liquid I.V. for a no-added-sugar / clean-label / quality-verified buyer.
5. **Formula distinction:** 1,000 mg potassium plus 380 mg sodium and 150 mg chloride shown as label facts, not physiological promises.
6. **Low-friction routine:** easy-tear stick / 12–16 oz mixing / take-it-with-you behavior.
7. **No-caffeine use case:** a mix for someone who wants hydration without choosing an energy drink.

> **Water rule:** Water appears only in the one long/outdoor heavy-sweat comparison asset, where its role is to create the precise, source-supported category gap. It does not appear in the apology, label-check, verdict, rating, tier, instruction, comparison, or Type 4 scripts.

> **Potassium rule:** The numerical panel is a selling fact in formula-comparison content. Medication or kidney context appears only in the dedicated instruction/correction asset, if the product, exact label, and use case are rechecked immediately before filming.

## Independent-review context and conflict boundary

Trail & Kale’s DryWater review describes a disclosed affiliate-code relationship. It corroborates **portability** and a less-salty taste preference for running/hiking use, while also noting that a low-calorie powder is not endurance carbohydrate fuel and that DryWater is lower in sodium than some hydration mixes. These are useful audience/use-case and objection leads, but the review’s affiliate relationship means it is not proof of the product’s efficacy or a universal ranking. [9]

The Live it Up dietitian-branded DryWater review is published by a competing hydration-product seller and directs readers to the publisher’s own alternative. It raises leads about price, taste/clumping, and sodium/potassium ratio, but it also conflicts with the current exact Raspberry Lemon ingredient panel by describing stevia leaf extract even though that ingredient does not appear in the exact current panel. It is therefore logged as a **conflicted competitor-source critique**, not as a factual basis for campaign claims, rankings, or product safety conclusions. [10]
