Evidence scope: In a small randomised crossover trial (12 young and 11 older euhydrated men) comparing 1 L water, fruit juice, a sports drink, and low-fat milk, sodium balance at three hours was negative after water/juice but neutral after sport drink/milk; the trial did not test DryWater, its formula, exercise, a 12–16-oz serving, or daily outcome claims: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37294517/. A 2024 narrative review of exercise hydration notes that fluid needs and beverage composition depend on exercise, environmental conditions, sweat loss, tolerance, and individual factors; sodium is central to fluid/electrolyte balance, while the evidence for added potassium’s independent water-retention effect remains equivocal: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10781183/. These sources can support ingredient/category education only, not the exact product’s advertised “cellular hydration,” performance, energy, cognition, mood, recovery, or immunity outcomes.

Safety scope: NIH ODS states that potassium is an essential intracellular nutrient, that adult adequate intake varies by sex/age (2,600–3,400 mg/day for healthy adults), and that the AI does not apply to people with impaired potassium excretion. ODS states that people with chronic kidney disease or use of ACE inhibitors, ARBs, or potassium-sparing diuretics can develop hyperkalemia even below the AI and should discuss total potassium intake with a clinician; severe hyperkalemia can cause life-threatening cardiac arrhythmias. The linked product’s 1,000-mg potassium dose and its own 1–2-stick daily suggestion must be viewed in that context, not as a diagnosis or medicine-replacement claim. NIH source: https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Potassium-HealthProfessional/.
