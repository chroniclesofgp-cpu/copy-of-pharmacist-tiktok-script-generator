## Universal Structural Rules (Apply to ALL Hooks)

These rules apply regardless of hook type:

1. **Gap Section is mandatory.** Every script must contain a "gap" — a statement of what most people are missing, doing wrong, or not aware of. The product fills this gap. Without the gap, the product recommendation feels like an ad rather than expert advice.

2. **Brand Close is required when an affiliate product is specified.** The brand close explains why THIS specific product/brand vs. a generic alternative. It must reference at least one specific differentiator (e.g., collagen brand #1 in the US, third-party tested, specific form of ingredient, clinical dose).

3. **Authority placement is hook-dependent.** Some hooks open with verbal authority ("As a pharmacist..."). Others use a visual badge overlay and go straight into the hook. See each hook's framework for the correct placement.

4. **Product introduction timing is late by default.** For education-heavy hooks (instruction-correction and suppressed-knowledge), the product should not appear until the final third of the script. Age-reversal is retired as a separate framework because the retained naturo example is comparison content and is now properly credited to the comparison framework. For symptom/pain-point hooks, the product can appear earlier once the cause is identified.

10. **Top-of-funnel scripts use a curiosity loop structure — product reveal comes last.** In TOF (top-of-funnel) scripts, the product is NEVER revealed until the final section. The structure is: hook the viewer → open a curiosity loop (what is causing this?) → close the first loop with education (here is why) → open a second loop (so what actually helps?) → close with the solution mechanism → THEN introduce the product as the thing that delivers that solution. Revealing the product early kills watch time because the viewer has no reason to keep watching. The product name, brand, and visual should not appear until the viewer has already been educated on the problem and the solution category. This applies to eligible symptom-reframe and suppressed-knowledge executions.

5. **CTA is always direct-cart-tap for TikTok Shop products.** "Link is in my shop below. Tap the cart." Soft CTAs ("check my profile") are only used when no TikTok Shop link is available.

6. **On-screen visuals are part of the script.** Each framework notes where infographics, study screenshots, or product shots should appear. These are not optional — they are structural elements that carry the educational weight and prevent scroll-off.

7. **Triple Hook Strategy is standard for every script.** Top-performing TikTok creators deploy three simultaneous attention triggers in the first 2 seconds:
   - **Visual hook:** What is physically on screen (product, face, prop, environment).
   - **Spoken/verbal hook:** The first words out of your mouth — the scroll-stopper.
   - **Text overlay hook:** On-screen text that appears simultaneously with the spoken hook. This must NOT repeat the spoken words verbatim — it must approach the same topic from a different angle to engage a second attention system.

   The tool generates 3 text overlay options for every script, each using a different angle:
   - **Question format** — e.g., "Why is your magnesium not working?"
   - **Provocative statement** — e.g., "Your pharmacist never told you this"
   - **Stakes framing** — e.g., "Most people are wasting their money"

   When building scripts manually (outside the tool), always generate all three text hook variants before filming. Pick the one that best matches your delivery energy for that video.

8. **Every script deliverable includes a caption and 5 hashtags.** This applies to both tool-generated scripts and manually written scripts.

   **Caption rules:**
   - Max 2 lines, conversational tone
   - Lead with the symptom, problem, or benefit — NOT the product name. "Dark underarms are not a hygiene issue" is correct. "Medicube kojic acid deodorant review" is wrong.
   - Subtle CTA acceptable (e.g., "definitely worth a try") but never transactional (e.g., "tap the orange cart")
   - **Tag the brand account in the caption text** when the product has a TikTok Shop presence (e.g., "@Medicube kojic acid deodorant is the one I recommend"). This is a collaboration/discovery signal — it notifies the brand and can trigger resharing. Confirmed pattern from rphreviews: he tags the brand in ~80% of captions.
   - **End every caption with the pharmacist disclaimer:** "Just a quick disclaimer: While I'm a licensed pharmacist, this is not medical advice." This is not just legal protection — it is a trust signal that reinforces the credential in the post copy. Confirmed pattern: rphreviews uses this in 100% of 34 analyzed captions without exception.

   **Hashtag rules:** exactly 5 — (1) brand name, (2) ingredient or product name, (3–4) pain point tags matching the hook's audience, (5) one general health/pharmacy tag.

   **Note on hashtag volume:** rphreviews uses hashtags in only ~9% of captions (3 of 34). Riva uses them in ~27% (6 of 22). Both rely primarily on natural-language SEO in the caption body rather than hashtag volume. The 5-hashtag rule is a safe default, but for captions with strong symptom-specific language, 0–3 hashtags is also a valid approach.

9. **New product scripts include 3–4 supporting PubMed studies.** Any time a script is written for a product not previously covered, include 3–4 relevant peer-reviewed citations with direct PubMed links. Studies should map to specific claims made in the script — mechanism studies for the education section, RCTs or systematic reviews for the gap/solution section. These are for the creator's reference and for on-screen study pop-ups.

### 1. `instruction-correction` — The Instruction / Correction Hook
**Reference Video:** @adoseofwellness (Riva) — 12.8M views
**Opening Line Pattern:** "Here's how to take [product/ingredient] the right way."
**Authority Placement:** Opening verbal — first sentence establishes pharmacist credential

**Section Sequence:**
```
Hook (light) → Authority (light) → Education/What It Is (heavy) → Gap (medium) → Solution/How To Use Correctly (medium) → Pain Point/Who This Helps (medium) → Product Introduction (medium) → Brand Close (light) → CTA (light)
```

**Section Weights & Content Rules:**
- **Hook:** One sentence. States the promise — "here is how to take X the right way" or "most people are taking X wrong."
- **Authority:** One sentence. "As a pharmacist..." or "I'm a pharmacist and I get asked about this constantly."
- **Education:** 2–3 sentences. What is this ingredient/product? What does it do in the body? Why does it matter? This is the heaviest section — it builds the trust that makes the recommendation credible.
- **Gap:** 1–2 sentences. What are most people missing? What common mistake undermines the product's effectiveness? This should be a specific, clinically accurate insight.
- **Solution/How To Use:** 1–2 specific usage tips. These must be universally applicable, not product-specific. Best timing, what to take it with, or what to avoid. Maximum two tips — do not lecture.
- **Pain Point/Who This Helps:** 1–2 sentences. Who is this for? Name the audience by symptom or situation.
- **Product Introduction:** 1–2 sentences. Introduce the specific product as the solution. Name the key differentiator.
- **Brand Close:** 1–2 sentences. Why this brand specifically. Must reference a specific, verifiable claim.
- **CTA:** "Link is in my shop below. Tap the cart and check the reviews."

**On-Screen Visuals (in order):**
1. Title text overlay — e.g., "How to Take [Ingredient] the Right Way"
2. Infographic: What [ingredient] does / importance
3. Study screenshot supporting a specific education claim
4. Infographic: Forms or types, if applicable
5. Product label close-up
6. Price/reviews overlay

**Key Structural Insight:** The first 70% of the video is pure education. The product appears only after the viewer already trusts the creator as an expert. By the time the product is mentioned, it feels like a recommendation from a trusted pharmacist, not an ad.

**PRODUCT REVEAL TIMING — CRITICAL CONSTRAINT:** The product must not be named, shown, or referenced until the final third of the script. The first two-thirds are pure education: what the ingredient does, why most people are using it wrong, and how to use it correctly. This is the defining structural difference between instruction-correction and right-way. In right-way, the product can appear mid-script. In instruction-correction, the product reveal is the payoff.

**Audience-Callout Variant (@rphreviews pattern):** Instead of a generic "you might be taking it wrong" opener, call out the specific audience and name one or two key benefits in the first three seconds before stating the correction.

Structure: *"So you are taking [PRODUCT], but you are not seeing [SPECIFIC BENEFIT 1] or [SPECIFIC BENEFIT 2]. Let me tell you what is going on — and here is how to take it the right way."*

### 5. `trend-or-trash` — The Trend or Trash Hook
**Reference Video:** @adoseofwellness (Riva) — 1.5M views
**Opening Line Pattern:** "Trend or trash, [category] edition — coming from a pharmacist."
**Authority Placement:** Opening verbal — credential immediately after hook phrase.

**Section Sequence:**
```
Hook (heavy) → Authority (light) → Tear-Down of Common/Popular Option (heavy) → Introduce Clinical Ingredient/Better Option (medium) → Product Reveal (medium) → Secondary Benefit (medium) → Price Justification (light) → CTA (light)
```

**Section Weights & Content Rules:**
- **Hook:** The “trend or trash” phrase plus category. It is polarizing and invites the viewer to find out whether their current choice survives the review.
- **Authority:** Brief credential—“coming from a pharmacist” or “as a pharmacist.”
- **Tear-Down:** Analyze a widely used, recognizable product or approach. Be specific about the type and the source-supported limitation; do not make unsupported accusations.
- **Clinical Ingredient:** Introduce the better ingredient or approach before the brand. This frames the sequence as education rather than a sponsored announcement.
- **Product Reveal:** Introduce the specific brand/product after the ingredient is established.
- **Secondary Benefit:** Add a distinct, source-supported benefit the viewer may not expect.
- **Price Justification:** Briefly explain value against the recognizable alternative without inventing price or offer conditions.
- **CTA:** Direct cart tap with a verified urgency condition.

**On-Screen Visuals:**
1. Static title card early
2. Dynamic captions throughout
3. Product bottle mid-video

**Key Structural Insight:** Introduce the ingredient before the brand. By the time the brand is revealed, the viewer has already understood why the ingredient/form matters.

**Section Sequence:**
```
Hook/Both Products Named (heavy) → Authority Signal (light) → Product A Breakdown (medium) → Product B Breakdown (medium) → Education/Why the Difference Matters (heavy) → Verdict Style Matched to Affiliate Structure (medium) → CTA (light)
```

**Section Weights & Content Rules:**
- **Hook:** Names BOTH products in the first 2 seconds. The question "which one is better?" is the hook — it creates immediate engagement because the viewer has likely seen or considered both.
- **Authority Signal:** One sentence. "I'm a pharmacist" or "I'm Dr. [Name], I don't gatekeep information." Delivered immediately after the hook question.
- **Product A Breakdown:** What does Product A contain? What is it best for? Be specific — name the key ingredients and their mechanisms. Fair, not dismissive.
- **Product B Breakdown:** Same treatment. Do not favor one product at this stage — the education is the value, not the verdict.
- **Education/Why the Difference Matters:** This is the heaviest section. Why do the ingredient differences matter clinically? What does each formula actually do in the body? This is where the pharmacist credential earns its keep.
- **Verdict Style Matched to Affiliate Structure:** Verdict style depends on affiliate structure. If only one of the two compared products is actually being sold, a decisive single winner is the proven pattern (rphreviews). If the creator sells or profits from both products, a nuanced split-by-use-case verdict is the proven pattern (Dr. Faith). If the two products are complementary rather than competing, reframe into the bundle variant (naturo/Riva).
- **CTA:** Direct cart tap for the recommended product, or both if applicable.

**On-Screen Visuals (in order):**
1. Both products shown on screen simultaneously — hold them up or show them side-by-side (early)
2. Ingredient/formula close-up for each product — show the label or key ingredient name as text overlay (mid)
3. Side-by-side comparison table or text overlay (mid)
4. "Best for [use case]" callout graphic — specific, not generic (late)
5. Product reviews screenshot (late, optional)

**Key Structural Insight:** The science does the selling, but the final verdict must match the affiliate structure. A decisive one-product verdict is appropriate where only one product is linked; a use-case split is appropriate when both are sold; and complementary products should resolve into the bundle variant rather than an artificial winner.

**Visual execution note (from rphreviews Rank 2, $89K GMV):** The strongest version of this hook is fully visual at 0:00 — both products physically held up in frame before a single word is spoken. The text overlay names both products. The verbal hook follows after. This visual-first approach stops the scroll before the viewer has processed any audio.

**The bundle variant — "A vs. B → You Need Both" (naturo, confirmed via direct transcript review in 1 of 5 creators' analyzed top-performing samples for this specific bundle resolution; two naturo source videos are retained):** When the two products are complementary rather than competing, the comparison can end with "actually, you should take both" — which drives a bundle purchase. This is the highest-converting variant because it doubles the cart value. The mid-video rehook for this variant is: "So, which one is better?" spoken and shown on screen — then the answer reveals they work together, not against each other.

The full structure for the bundle variant:
```
False choice hook ("A vs. B — which one?") → Educate on A's mechanism (heavy) → Educate on B's mechanism (heavy) → Synergy reveal at 70-75% ("actually, they work together") → Bundle product reveal → Proof stack → CTA
```

This structure captures search intent from BOTH "A" and "B" audiences, eliminates the need to choose (removes a purchase barrier), and naturally sets up a higher-ticket bundle recommendation. naturo's NMN vs. Astaxanthin videos using this structure average $71K GMV each.

**The split-by-use-case verdict formula (Dr. Faith):** "Product A is better for [specific use case]. But Product B is better for [broader use case] and has more ingredients." Use this only when the creator sells or profits from both products. The losing product must still be presented fairly.

**The brand comparison variant (Dr. Faith, Rank 6, $30K GMV):** "Brand X vs. Brand Y — same ingredient, which one did it better?" This variant is used when both products contain the same key ingredient but differ in formulation, dose, or quality. The verdict is based on ingredient quality criteria (form, dose, certifications) rather than mechanism differences. The creator's recommended brand wins on quality grounds, not on ingredient superiority.

### 9. `warning-signs` — The Warning Signs Hook
**Reference Video:** @faithfuldoc — 6M views
**Opening Line Pattern:** "If you have [symptom], this is a warning sign." / "Stop ignoring these signs."
**Authority Placement:** Opening visual badge — NOT verbal opening

**Section Sequence:**
```
Hook (heavy) → Baseline/Normal vs. Abnormal (heavy) → Catalyst/What Causes This (light) → Conflict/Why It's Dangerous or Problematic (medium) → Consequence/What Happens If Ignored (heavy) → Reassurance + Solution (light) → CTA (light)
```

**Section Weights & Content Rules:**
- **Hook:** Names the warning sign. Creates urgency — viewer needs to know if this applies to them.
- **Baseline:** What is normal? What is the warning sign? This section is heavy because it helps the viewer self-diagnose.
- **Catalyst:** What causes this condition? Brief.
- **Conflict:** Why is this a problem? What does it indicate?
- **Consequence:** What happens if this is ignored? This is the heaviest section — it creates urgency to act.
- **Reassurance + Solution:** "The good news is..." — remove shame, provide a solution. Product introduced here if applicable.
- **CTA:** Soft or direct depending on product availability.

**On-Screen Visuals:**
1. Reaction video or screenshot (early) — borrowed authority
2. Medical reference screenshots (throughout) — WebMD, Healthline, PubMed
3. Framed document or letter board (throughout)

### 27. `viral-metaphor` — The Viral Metaphor Hook

**Funnel Stage:** TOF (primary) — works on cold audiences who have never heard of the product or ingredient

**Psychological Mechanism:** Opens with a surprising, entertaining, or emotionally resonant third-party story — from nature, history, science, or viral news — that has an unexpected metaphorical connection to the supplement's mechanism. The viewer engages with the story before realizing it is a supplement video. By the time the product is mentioned, the viewer has already accepted the core premise (e.g., "nature has been using this compound for millions of years") and is primed to believe the product works.

**What makes it distinct from other hooks:**
- Unlike the `fear-external-threat` hook (Framework 25), this hook uses a *positive or surprising* story — not a threat. The emotional tone is wonder/curiosity, not fear.
- Unlike the `storytime` hook, the story is *third-party* (not personal). The creator is not the protagonist.
- Unlike the `expert-verdict` hook, the hook does not open with a conclusion. It opens with a story that leads to a conclusion.
- This is the only hook in this framework set that is fully TOF-capable — a cold viewer who has never thought about supplements can engage with a viral animal story.

**Section Sequence:**
```
Viral story hook (0–20s) → Bridge to mechanism (3–5s) → Mechanism/science section (30–45s) → Product reveal (at 60–70% mark) → Proof stack (10–15s) → Objection handle (5–10s) → CTA (15–20s)
```

**Section Weights & Content Rules:**
- **Viral story hook (0–20s):** Open with a surprising fact, discovery, or story from nature/history/science. No supplement mention. No product visible. The story must be genuinely interesting on its own — it should feel like a nature documentary or science clip, not a setup for a sales pitch. The story must have a clear metaphorical connection to the supplement's mechanism (e.g., orangutan using medicinal plants → humans should use plant medicine).
- **Bridge to mechanism (3–5s):** One sentence that pivots from the story to the human application. "Turns out, humans have been using this same compound for centuries — and modern science just confirmed why it works." Keep the bridge short and natural — do not over-explain the metaphor.
- **Mechanism/science section (30–45s):** Standard mechanism section. Name the ingredient, explain the mechanism, show 3–5 study screenshots. This section is identical to other hook types — the story hook only changes the opening, not the education architecture.
- **Product reveal (at 60–70% mark):** Standard product reveal. Hold product up, name the brand, explain why this specific product.
- **Proof stack (10–15s):** Quality certifications, ingredient label, review count.
- **Objection handle (5–10s):** Dosage, timing, interactions.
- **CTA (15–20s):** Standard scarcity CTA.

**Verbatim hook examples (from Drew @drew.review1):**
```
VERBAL: "Did you know that wild orangutans in Borneo have been observed applying medicinal plants to their wounds? Scientists recently discovered they were using Akar Kuning — a plant that contains berberine — the same compound that's now being studied for blood sugar regulation, inflammation, and gut health in humans."
```
```
TEXT HOOK: "Wild orangutans discovered this before we did 🦧" or "Nature figured this out millions of years before scientists did"
```

**Key structural insight:** The viral story hook is the only format where the first 15–25 seconds contain zero supplement content. This is its greatest strength and its greatest risk. Strength: it bypasses TikTok Shop fatigue — the viewer does not realize they are watching a supplement video until they are already engaged. Risk: if the story is not genuinely interesting, the viewer scrolls before the supplement content begins. The story must be able to stand alone as interesting content.

**Narrative loop technique:** The highest-performing version of this hook closes the narrative loop in the CTA — referencing the opening story one final time. Drew's berberine video ends: "So if you want to be smart like the orangutan..." This rewards viewers who watched from the beginning and creates a satisfying sense of completion.

**Best for:**
- Ingredients with a natural origin story (plant-based, traditional medicine, evolutionary history)
- Products targeting audiences who are skeptical of supplements (the story bypasses the skepticism)
- TOF videos on products the audience has never heard of
- Second or third videos on a product where the standard hooks have already been used

**Audience variants:**
1. **Nature/animal story** — "Wild orangutans discovered this before we did" (berberine, turmeric, ashwagandha)
2. **Historical/ancient use** — "Ancient Egyptians used this compound 3,000 years ago" (astaxanthin from algae, collagen from bone broth)
3. **Surprising science discovery** — "Scientists studying deep-sea organisms just found the most powerful antioxidant on Earth" (astaxanthin)

**GMV evidence:** Drew's berberine video using this hook: $55,000 GMV (Acct2 Rank 5). Only 2 examples in Drew's catalog — both in $50K+ tier. High ceiling, limited data.

### 28. `side-effect-surprise` — The Side Effect / Unexpected Bonus Hook

**Opening Pattern:** Opens with an unexpected, desirable *side effect* or *bonus outcome* rather than the intended use. Places the viewer in a second-person scenario where they already have the product and are surprised by a benefit they didn't expect.
**Funnel Stage:** TOF/MOF — works for both cold audiences (the surprise is the hook) and warm audiences (who may have already heard of the product for its primary use but not the bonus benefit)
**Tier:** Tier 2 — Situational (confirmed via direct transcript review in 1 of 5 creators' analyzed top-performing samples (90-day window), $216K top GMV — high ceiling, limited cross-creator data)

**Section Sequence:**
```
Surprise scenario (light) → Credential (light) → Explain the unexpected mechanism (heavy) → Bridge to primary use (medium) → Product reveal (medium) → Proof stack (medium) → CTA (light)
```

**Section Weights & Content Rules:**

The viewer does not need to have taken the product — the second-person framing invites them into the scenario. The surprise must be genuinely desirable (glowing skin, unexpected energy, better sleep when they were only trying to fix X). Duration: 3–5 seconds.

- **Explain the unexpected mechanism (0:10–0:45):** This is the heaviest section. Why does this product produce this unexpected benefit? The mechanism explanation must be specific and clinical — it is what converts the surprise into a purchase driver. For astaxanthin: "Astaxanthin is a carotenoid — the same pigment that gives salmon and flamingos their pink color. When you take it, it deposits in your skin and acts as an internal sunscreen, giving you a natural warmth and glow." Duration: 25–40 seconds.
- **Bridge to primary use (0:45–0:55):** After explaining the unexpected benefit, bridge back to the primary use. "And that's on top of what it's actually known for — [primary use]." This section validates the viewer who came for the primary use AND the viewer who came for the bonus benefit. Duration: 8–12 seconds.
- **Product reveal (at 65–75% mark):** Standard product reveal. Hold product up, name the brand, explain why this specific product. Duration: 5–10 seconds.
- **Proof stack (10–15s):** Quality certifications, ingredient label, review count. Include at least one review that mentions the unexpected benefit — this validates that the surprise is real and repeatable, not just the creator's experience.
- **CTA (15–20s):** Standard scarcity CTA. "I'll link it below — snag it while they still have it in stock."

**Key Structural Insight:** This hook is the only format in the library that leads with a positive surprise rather than a problem, fear, or question. Every other hook activates a pain point or information gap. This hook activates delight — the viewer is not trying to solve a problem, they are discovering a bonus they didn't know existed. This makes it uniquely effective for products the viewer has already dismissed ("I tried that for X and it didn't work") — the surprise benefit reactivates interest from a completely different angle.

**The second-person scenario is critical:** "You took astaxanthin for back pain" is more powerful than "I took astaxanthin for back pain" (personal story) or "People who take astaxanthin for back pain" (third-person). Second-person places the viewer inside the experience before they have consciously decided to engage.

### 29. `scam-warning` — The "This Is Fake" / Scam Warning / Quality Differentiation Hook

**Hook ID:** `scam-warning`
**Tier:** Tier 2 Situational — **Validated (1 creator, 4 videos, consistent structure)**. Needs 2+ additional healthcare creators to reach universal rule status.
**Format:** Consumer protection PSA hybrid — 60–75 seconds
**Funnel Stage:** BOF → MOF. Targets viewers who have already seen the viral product and are in the purchase decision phase. Also captures TOF viewers who have not yet seen the product but are drawn in by the scam-warning framing.

---

**What Makes This Hook Work:**

This hook exploits the psychological phenomenon of negative contrast — the viewer is shown what is wrong before they are shown what is right. The brain is wired to pay more attention to threats than to opportunities. By opening with fake/counterfeit products and the word "fake," the creator triggers a threat-detection response that is almost impossible to scroll past. The viewer stays not because they want to buy the product, but because they want to know if they are at risk.

The hook also activates a secondary mechanism: social proof by proxy. The fact that a product has so many counterfeits is itself evidence that it went viral and that real people are buying it. The creator never has to say "this product is popular" — the existence of fake listings says it for them.

**Critical structural distinction from the "Fake Companies Moat" technique:** The "Fake Companies Moat" (documented in Riva's deep analysis as Candidate 3) is a post-reveal objection-handling technique — the creator mentions fake companies *after* showing the real product. This hook is structurally different: the fake products ARE the opening visual, appearing in the first 0–3 seconds before the real product is shown. The sequence is inverted: fake first → real product as the reveal. This inversion is what makes it a hook, not just a moat.

### 30. `split-screen-comparison` — Split-Screen Comparison
**Hook ID:** `split-screen-comparison`

**What Makes This Hook Work:**

The "who wins?" curiosity gap is the primary retention mechanic. Viewers stay to see the outcome of the debate. The rapid-fire back-and-forth rhythm is hypnotic — the alternating dialogue creates a pace that is difficult to scroll past.

**Section Weights & Content Rules:**

- **Hook (0:00–0:05):** Both people/products visible immediately. Text hook appears at frame 1. The worse-alternative person must start first — they are the setup.
- **Rapid-fire comparison (0:05–0:30):** 3–5 rounds minimum. Each round: worse alternative states a feature/ingredient → better product counters with the same single product. Zero dead air. The rhythm is the hook — do not slow down.
- **The reveal moment (~0:30):** The worse-alternative person must ask "what do you have?" — this is the organic product reveal. Do not reveal the product name before this moment.
- **Pharmacist education (0:30–0:50):** This is the pharmacist-exclusive section. One mechanism insight (why the combined form absorbs better, why the specific strain matters, why the formulation is superior). One clinical note or study reference. Keep it to 2–3 sentences — this is not an education video, it is a comparison video.

**Pharmacist Adaptation Notes:**

1. **Role assignment:** Pharmacist = better product (authority). Wife/partner = worse alternative (common folk with the complicated routine). This dynamic is natural and relatable.
2. **The education section is the pharmacist's unique contribution.** The original creators skip this entirely. Adding 2–3 sentences of mechanism explanation after the reveal is what makes the pharmacist version more credible and more educational than the lifestyle creator version.

4. **Compliance:** No diagnostic claims in the comparison rounds. "I've got [ingredient] for [benefit]" is fine. "I've got [ingredient] to treat [condition]" is not.
5. **Format A (solo):** Film yourself twice — once in one outfit/side, once in another. Edit as split screen. The "arguing with yourself" visual is the scroll-stop mechanic.
6. **Format B (with wife):** Wife plays the person with the complicated routine. She should appear frustrated, confused, or exasperated — not defeated. The energy is playful, not mean.

### 31. `tier-list-comparison` — Tier-List Comparison
**Creator Source:** @flashfindstiktok0 (viral TikTok Shop video, 2026) — adapted for pharmacist authority  
**Active status:** **Active/situational.** Revalidated in `analysis/rebuild-frameworks/TIER_LIST_COMPARISON_REVALIDATION_CARD_2026-09-05.md`. The direct source basis is one creator; use sample-scoped wording and do not imply broad cross-creator validation.
**Tier:** Tier 1 Situational  
**Format:** TOF / MOF (60–90 sec)  
**Source/Validation:** One preserved creator reference plus approved pharmacist adaptation rules for clinical neutrality and staged reactions.  

**Verified Verbal Hook Pattern:**
> *"Ranking every major [product/treatment category] from F-tier to S-tier — as a pharmacist."*

**Verified Text Hook Pattern:**
> *"Ranking [Category] (named ingredients, formats, or treatments) — S-Tier revealed 👀"*

**Section Sequence:**
```
Tier-List Setup Hook (light) → Credential (light) → Named F/D/C/B/A-tier items in deliberately nonsequential order (medium) → Named S-tier product reveal + mechanism (heavy) → Application/Versatility Range (light) → Scarcity CTA (light)
```

**Section Weights & Content Rules:**
- **Hook & Credential (0:00–0:07):** State the tier-list ranking immediately while wearing a white coat or in a clinic setting. Establish pharmacist authority right after the hook.
- **Alternative Breakdown (0:07–0:35):** Rank existing, well-known **named ingredients, formats, or treatments**. The off-camera person must say the actual item first (for example, “magnesium glycinate?”); the pharmacist reacts with the tier and one specific, clinically grounded reason. Use the full F-through-S scale when warranted. Intermediate ranks must deliberately jump (for example, A → F → C → B), rather than climb predictably toward S.
- **S-Tier Reveal (0:35–0:55):** The off-camera person says the actual hero product or named product architecture—not “S-tier product.” The pharmacist then reacts “S tier” and brings the product into frame. S is always the final ranked item and is followed by the simplified mechanism and published evidence where available.
- **Application Range (0:55–1:10):** Broaden the utility — explain where else it applies (hairline, crown, beard, eyebrows).
- **Scarcity CTA (1:10–1:20):** Address stock availability and direct to the orange cart.

**Pharmacist Adaptation Notes:**
1. **Clinical Neutrality:** Unlike lifestyle creators who dismiss drugs entirely, the pharmacist version acknowledges that older treatments *do* work (e.g., minoxidil does grow hair), but explains their compliance or biochemical limitations honestly. This builds unmatched trust.
2. **Built-in Objection Handling:** By tearing down existing treatments first, you eliminate all buyer hesitation. When you introduce the product, the viewer's mental objections have already been answered.
3. **The S-Tier Pivot:** The dramatic shift to S-tier creates a psychological peak. The viewer feels they have unlocked an insider secret.
4. **Staged Reaction Rule:** Never ask the off-camera person to say a generic tier placeholder. Every prompt must name the actual ingredient, product format, treatment, or hero product; the pharmacist assigns the grade on camera.
5. **Unpredictable Ladder Rule:** F is available for an option that is genuinely poor for the stated goal. Do not use a tidy C → B → A climb; only S is fixed last because it transitions into the product reveal.
